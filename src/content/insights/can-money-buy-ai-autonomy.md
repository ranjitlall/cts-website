In April 2024, Microsoft announced a $1.5 billion investment in G42<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1">1</a></sup>, an Abu Dhabi-based technology company seeking to expand its AI business. The agreement followed negotiations over G42's relationships with Chinese technology companies, particularly Huawei. As Steve Feldstein explains in his book *Bytes and Bullets*<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2">2</a></sup>, American officials were concerned that these relationships could undermine restrictions on China's access to advanced technologies. G42 reduced its cooperation with Chinese suppliers and entered into a partnership that gave Microsoft a minority stake in the Emirati company.

The negotiations illustrate the opportunities and constraints facing middle powers seeking technological autonomy. The UAE gained access to American technology and a partner capable of supporting its domestic AI industry. Washington advanced its effort to limit Chinese technological influence in the Gulf. Although both sides benefited, the technologies involved remained subject to American export regulations. That condition still applies. Under a July 2026 rule, G42's license-free access to advanced American chips expires on April 6, 2027, unless the company becomes a U.S. company or receives further authorization.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-3" id="fnref-3">3</a></sup> This raises a broader question concerning the extent to which financial resources and international partnerships provide control over the technologies that countries seek to acquire.

Saudi Arabia and the UAE can finance computing facilities on a scale that few other middle powers can match. However, neither produces the advanced processors required for these facilities. In this piece, I examine technological autonomy through three related dimensions. The first concerns obtaining advanced computing equipment on reliable terms. The second concerns domestic control over the operation and use of computing infrastructure. The third concerns the security and continuity of computing services during military disruption.

The piece argues that Gulf investments have strengthened the bargaining position of Saudi Arabia and the UAE by attracting leading technology companies and improving access to advanced computing equipment. However, partnerships with competing American suppliers leave both countries dependent on the government regulating their exports. Thus, Washington continues to influence the expansion and upgrading of domestic facilities. The Iran war has also demonstrated that building infrastructure at home does not guarantee that it will keep running, and has revealed the importance of critical infrastructure security. Technological autonomy in this sense does not require producing every component domestically. It concerns the conditions under which domestic institutions can obtain and continue using technologies developed elsewhere.

## Developing AI infrastructure in the Gulf
Saudi Arabia and the UAE have attracted technology companies by offering capital, land, electricity and supporting infrastructure.

In May 2025, G42, OpenAI, Oracle, NVIDIA, Cisco and SoftBank announced Stargate UAE<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-4" id="fnref-4">4</a></sup>, a planned one-gigawatt computing cluster within a proposed five-gigawatt campus in Abu Dhabi. G42 would build the cluster, while OpenAI and Oracle would operate it. The partners expected the first 200 megawatts to become operational in 2026. The arrangement combines Emirati investment with foreign equipment and operational expertise. Also, it shows why the location of a facility and responsibility for operating it should be examined separately. The announced division of responsibilities provides information about the companies involved, although the full commercial terms are not public.

Saudi Arabia has pursued a similar objective through HUMAIN, which its Public Investment Fund established in May 2025<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-5" id="fnref-5">5</a></sup>. HUMAIN has developed partnerships with AMD, Cisco and NVIDIA.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-6" id="fnref-6">6</a></sup> In August 2026, AMD, Cisco and HUMAIN announced<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-7" id="fnref-7">7</a></sup> that systems using AMD Instinct MI355X processors had entered production. They also outlined additional capacity beginning in 2027 and an objective of reaching one gigawatt by 2030. Moreover, HUMAIN is expected to develop domestic AI services and models alongside its computing facilities. The success of this effort will depend partly on whether its international partnerships support local technical capabilities as well as infrastructure construction.

Domestic institutions have also developed AI capabilities beyond financing computing facilities. In the UAE, G42's Core42 operates sovereign cloud and AI infrastructure<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-8" id="fnref-8">8</a></sup>, while Abu Dhabi's Technology Innovation Institute developed the open-source Falcon model family<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9">9</a></sup>. In Saudi Arabia, HUMAIN launched HUMAIN Chat in August 2025, using its ALLaM 34B Arabic-language model on Saudi-hosted infrastructure<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-10" id="fnref-10">10</a></sup>. These initiatives demonstrate domestic experience in providing cloud services and developing AI models. The extent to which new computing investments strengthen these capabilities also depends on the access they provide to domestic researchers, firms and public institutions.

## Export controls and access to advanced technology
Farrell and Newman (2019), in their influential study of weaponized interdependence<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-11" id="fnref-11">11</a></sup>, explain how states occupying important positions within international networks can exercise influence over others. I think their argument is relevant to advanced AI processors, which are supplied by a relatively small number of companies and remain subject to export regulations.

The G42 negotiations show how these conditions influence commercial relationships. Feldstein shows that American officials feared Beijing could use G42 to siphon advanced U.S. chips to China, and that Chinese firms could gain access to the genomic data of millions of Americans through G42's DNA sequencing project. National security adviser Jake Sullivan raised these concerns with Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed in June 2023, and Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo later told him that G42 had to choose a side. By the end of 2023, G42 had agreed to sever its Chinese relationships and phase out Huawei hardware, and in return it was assured continued access to NVIDIA chips. Its Chinese investments, however, moved to Lunate, a new fund also controlled by Sheikh Tahnoon. Then the Biden administration helped G42 find an American partner, and Raimondo was closely involved in the negotiations with Microsoft.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2-2">2</a></sup> Under the April 2024 agreement, G42 committed to using Microsoft Azure for its AI applications and services, alongside security commitments developed in consultation with both governments.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1-2">1</a></sup> Mulani and Fort<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-12" id="fnref-12">12</a></sup> also doubt how far this decoupling has gone. They note that e&, the UAE's state-owned telecommunications company, continues to work with both Microsoft and Huawei. They also suggest that the UAE's partnerships with the United States reflect a pragmatic strategy to hedge bets on which power will prevail in the AI race.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-12" id="fnref-12-2">12</a></sup>

On November 19, 2025, the U.S. Department of Commerce authorized<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-13" id="fnref-13">13</a></sup> G42 and Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN to purchase advanced computing equipment equivalent to up to 35,000 NVIDIA GB300 processors each. Both approvals carried security and reporting requirements. The decision expanded access for the two countries while demonstrating that purchasing power alone did not determine which technologies they could obtain.

The UAE subsequently received more favorable treatment. A July 2026 final rule<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-3" id="fnref-3-2">3</a></sup> moved the country from Country Groups D:3 and D:4 to A:5, expanding eligibility for specified exports and license exceptions. The Commerce Department's accompanying announcement<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-14" id="fnref-14">14</a></sup> described the revised framework. Under the rule, G42 and Core42, a G42 subsidiary, could receive certain advanced computing items without individual export licenses.

Nevertheless, the authorization for G42 and Core42 is scheduled to expire on April 6, 2027, unless the Bureau of Industry and Security issues a subsequent notice. If the two companies have not become U.S. companies by that date, they will need to apply for authorization through the bureau's advisory-opinion process to maintain their approved status. The rule sets no expiry date for the eight U.S.-headquartered AI companies it approves, including Microsoft, Oracle and OpenAI. These companies and their UAE subsidiaries may also use License Exception Strategic Trade Authorization, which is not available to G42 and Core42.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-3" id="fnref-3-3">3</a></sup> This means the rule provides different conditions for Emirati and American companies operating in the same country.

The July changes did not extend to Saudi Arabia, whose HUMAIN continued to rely on its company-specific approval. The July rule did not affect HUMAIN's November 2025 authorization. G42 and HUMAIN received comparable authorizations in November 2025 but subsequently faced different regulatory arrangements. Because the July regulation governs future transfers of advanced computing items, it also shapes access to the equipment required to upgrade and expand computing systems. For investments extending over many years, the terms governing future transfers may be as important as the initial authorization. Computing facilities require successive generations of processors and networking equipment. A government may possess considerable bargaining power when financing a new project while remaining dependent on regulatory decisions when the project needs expansion or replacement equipment.

## International partnerships and domestic capabilities
Dependence on foreign technology does not remove the bargaining opportunities available to Gulf governments. Building large computing facilities requires capital, electricity, land and access to markets. As Phillips-Robins, Tawil and Winter-Levy<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-15" id="fnref-15">15</a></sup> suggest, countries able to attract computing investment can negotiate for improved access to advanced AI. The same authors argue that leverage over semiconductor supply chain chokepoints is especially valuable. Hosting computing infrastructure also generates bargaining power, although it does not provide the same leverage as controlling scarce semiconductor technologies.

The Microsoft-G42 agreement shows the benefits and costs of these partnerships. G42 obtained access to Microsoft's advanced GPUs and permission to sell Microsoft services, while Microsoft gained an ownership stake and a partner that could help it enter markets in Asia, Africa and Latin America and compete with Chinese cloud providers.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2-3">2</a></sup> Feldstein explains that Microsoft expected the agreement to "lock in" the UAE into its technical ecosystem, because the switching costs of transferring data and rebuilding software infrastructure around another provider would deter customers from leaving.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2-4">2</a></sup> Once a firm builds its applications and staff expertise around one platform, moving to another becomes costly. The benefits of a partnership can be assessed together with the ability of domestic firms to maintain and adapt their services if commercial circumstances change.

It is possible to argue that operational arrangements are equally important. The Stargate UAE plan assigns construction to G42 and operation to OpenAI and Oracle.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-4" id="fnref-4-2">4</a></sup> Public announcements provide limited information about how these companies share decisions concerning computing capacity and how much will be available to domestic researchers and firms. HUMAIN's October 2026 expansion with NVIDIA, for example, involves 84 megawatts of planned computing capacity, with 50 megawatts dedicated to an unnamed American customer.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-16" id="fnref-16">16</a></sup> These arrangements show how domestic cloud services and model development can coexist with new computing projects whose operating responsibilities and customer allocations involve foreign companies. Access for local researchers and firms consequently depends partly on those commercial arrangements.

Gulf governments can reduce these dependencies in two ways. The Tony Blair Institute for Global Change<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-17" id="fnref-17">17</a></sup> argues that middle powers can improve their technological position by investing in open-source software and interoperable systems, and by using procurement to require interoperability. Such capabilities may reduce switching costs and make domestic applications less dependent on individual providers. Similarly, Saudi partnerships with AMD and NVIDIA can expand procurement options, although both companies remain subject to American export controls. Jagtiani and Kausch<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-18" id="fnref-18">18</a></sup> characterize middle power agency in AI as meaningful but limited. Both options fit this description, because they reduce dependence on individual companies while leaving export decisions in American hands.

Consequently, the value of Gulf partnerships depends partly on the domestic capabilities they help develop. Interoperable software may ease movement between cloud providers, but it arguably cannot secure the processors on which those providers depend. Financial resources can secure investments and commercial agreements, but long-term autonomy also requires operational expertise and some flexibility over technology suppliers.

## The Iran war and infrastructure security
The Iran war has exposed another vulnerability associated with computing infrastructure in the Gulf. Following American and Israeli strikes against Iran on February 28, 2026, Iranian retaliatory attacks damaged Amazon Web Services facilities in the region. AWS reported that two facilities in the UAE were directly struck, while infrastructure in Bahrain was also damaged.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-19" id="fnref-19">19</a></sup> These attacks disrupted services used by businesses and other institutions. The incidents were especially consequential because cloud services support activities beyond the facilities themselves. Damage at one location can affect clients and institutions that rely on the same regional infrastructure.

In its September 15 updates,<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-19" id="fnref-19-2">19</a></sup> AWS reported that it could not restore access to resources and data hosted exclusively in its Bahrain cloud region. It reached the same conclusion for one of its three UAE availability zones, while recovery continued in the other two. The damage showed that even established commercial cloud systems could experience prolonged disruption.

The AWS attacks damaged commercial cloud facilities, but Iran also signaled that the new AI projects were potential targets. In April 2026, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps threatened the planned Stargate UAE campus in Abu Dhabi.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-20" id="fnref-20">20</a></sup> In September, Reuters reported<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-21" id="fnref-21">21</a></sup> that the UAE was considering revisions to its planned five-gigawatt campus, including dispersed sites and additional protection against drone and missile attacks. The initial Stargate UAE phase was expected to proceed with modifications. Saudi Arabia faces similar risks. A Houthi attack on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport on October 10, 2026, killed 13 people. Saudi Arabia subsequently postponed its Future Investment Initiative conference.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-22" id="fnref-22">22</a></sup>

The war has also influenced investment decisions and the design of infrastructure protection. The International Institute for Strategic Studies reports that Pure Data Centres Group paused investment decisions following attacks on regional data centers, while other major technology investments continued. The IISS identifies underground server installations, reinforced generator facilities and additional server redundancy as measures for protecting AI infrastructure<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-23" id="fnref-23">23</a></sup>. These measures address the ability to maintain computing services when individual sites are damaged, alongside the cost of building and protecting new facilities.

Assessments of the longer-term consequences differ. Aaron Bartnick<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-24" id="fnref-24">24</a></sup> argues that Washington's decision to encourage American companies to build AI infrastructure in the Gulf was a mistake and that companies should reconsider projects still in their planning stages. Justin Alexander<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-25" id="fnref-25">25</a></sup> argues that the Gulf can remain an important AI hub because its advantages in capital and energy persist despite the war. Gulf states may continue to attract investment while facing higher costs for protecting facilities and ensuring continuity.

For technological autonomy, the question is whether domestic institutions can maintain computing services during disruptions. Doing so requires the ability to restore data and transfer workloads when a particular site is unavailable. Backup facilities and distributed infrastructure may improve resilience, although reliance on facilities abroad would introduce additional dependence on foreign providers and jurisdictions.

## Implications for technological autonomy
The Gulf experience suggests that computing investments should be assessed by the capabilities available to domestic institutions. The three capabilities examined in this piece provide a basis for this assessment, and each points to a priority for Gulf governments.

First, Gulf governments can seek more predictable access to advanced processors and future upgrades. Second, partnerships should provide domestic institutions with computing resources and opportunities to acquire technical expertise, building on local experience in cloud services and model development and extending it to operating large computing facilities. Third, governments should plan for disruptions through backup capacity and recovery arrangements that do not depend on a single facility or provider. These measures may involve additional costs and cooperation with foreign companies, but they can improve the reliability of domestic services. They also provide criteria for evaluating whether big investment announcements translate into capabilities available to local institutions.

These priorities do not require Saudi Arabia or the UAE to reproduce the entire semiconductor supply chain. International specialization makes such an objective costly and potentially unnecessary. Gulf countries have shown that capital and infrastructure investment can attract leading technology companies. The G42 negotiations and the different export arrangements governing Saudi and Emirati firms show that partnerships with competing suppliers do not eliminate dependence on American regulatory authority. The Iran war has also demonstrated the physical vulnerability of regional computing infrastructure. Gulf investments can expand domestic capacity and improve commercial bargaining terms, but Washington continues to influence future technology access, while regional conflict can disrupt facilities that Gulf governments have financed and built.
