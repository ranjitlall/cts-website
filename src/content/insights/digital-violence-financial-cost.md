## Abuse that follows the money

In 2020, the Commonwealth Bank of Australia found that, over three months, more than 8,000 of its customers had received multiple low-value deposits, often worth less than a dollar, with potentially abusive messages written in the transaction descriptions. The content ranged from profanity to “clear references to domestic and family violence”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-1" id="fnref-1-1">1</a></sup> Westpac later reported that people targeted in this way had often already blocked the sender on social media and by phone, and that some senders had “circumvented the AVO [apprehended violence order] by using payments”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2-1">2</a></sup>

Each system involved operated within its own boundary. The social media platform blocked an account, the phone network blocked a number and the payment system executed valid transfers. The person targeted experienced one continuous pattern of abuse that moved from one system to the next. The banks responded within their own systems, introducing acceptable-use policies, blocking payments and closing perpetrators’ accounts; Westpac alone blocked more than 6,000 payments.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-2" id="fnref-2-2">2</a></sup> Each institution primarily responded within its own channel, while the pattern of abuse ran across them.

This is how digital violence turns into economic harm. Abuse that starts online travels across systems, and its costs settle on the person targeted, often in places where the platform’s safeguards no longer apply. It also illustrates the accountability challenge that interactionality, discussed below, brings into view: when harm crosses institutional boundaries, the ability to recognise, interrupt and remedy it may not travel with it.

## From online abuse to lost income

The economic consequences of technology-facilitated gender-based violence are documented, though rarely measured at scale. In Brazil, the Project Leaked study surveyed 141 people affected by the non-consensual sharing of intimate images. Six per cent reported losing a job, 5% had difficulty finding work and 16% paid for counselling or psychiatric treatment.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-3" id="fnref-3-1">3</a></sup> A peer-reviewed scoping review of research in low- and middle-income countries includes these figures in its account of the financial impact of technology-facilitated sexual violence.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-4" id="fnref-4-1">4</a></sup> Each is a cost borne privately. AI-generated sexual deepfakes extend this harm. Fabricated intimate content can now be created with one system and circulated through others without any authentic image existing, with the same potential for reputational damage, professional exclusion and lost income. The Brazilian figures predate the current wave of generative AI, and the economic consequences of AI-generated sexual deepfakes remain inadequately measured.

In Malawi, a survey of 67 low-income women market traders in Karonga district found that 76% reported a loss of income as a result of cyber violence, and smaller numbers reported losing a home or educational opportunities.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-5" id="fnref-5-1">5</a></sup> The sample is small and drawn from a single market. For women whose trade depends on a phone and on social media, abuse that reaches those channels reaches their livelihood.

Professional withdrawal carries costs of its own. In a 2026 global survey by UN Women and partners, 45% of women journalists said they self-censor on social media to avoid abuse, and nearly 22% said they self-censor in their jobs. Only 14% had taken action against perpetrators, enablers or employers.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-6" id="fnref-6-1">6</a></sup> The same dynamic can affect women across sectors, from finance, economics and science to the media and public life: wherever work depends on visibility, stepping back from it has an economic price.

Economic losses may also follow when women lose access to the platforms on which their visibility or income depends. Wrongful account restrictions, demonetisation and reputational damage can interrupt earnings, business relationships and professional opportunities, even after the underlying abuse has been addressed.

Some of the largest costs never appear as a reportable incident. Women may reduce their public presence, leave a role or decide not to enter public or political life at all. In Australia, a Women for Election survey of 110 women in public office or standing for election found that 89% had experienced online abuse linked to their role, 82% had changed their behaviour because of it and half questioned whether they would run again. Nearly two-thirds (65%) had personally experienced or witnessed AI-generated abuse, deepfakes or manipulated content targeting women in public life. Nearly all (94%) believed online abuse was discouraging capable women from entering politics.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-7" id="fnref-7-1">7</a></sup> The income and opportunities forgone in this way are recorded nowhere, which is why this chilling effect needs to be treated as an economic harm in its own right. The losses can include professional opportunities never pursued, businesses never established and careers abandoned before their economic consequences can be measured.

At the aggregate level, the European Parliamentary Research Service has estimated the cost of gender-based cyber violence to individuals and society in the European Union, much of it reflecting reduced quality of life, at €49.0 billion to €89.3 billion a year.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-8" id="fnref-8-1">8</a></sup> A 2026 study for the European Parliament reports that victims experience “financial costs and withdrawal from work and public life”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-1">9</a></sup> The World Bank notes that weak legal protection against gender-based violence imposes economic costs through reduced productivity and lost earnings.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-10" id="fnref-10-1">10</a></sup>

## Why the costs stay hidden

These figures come from small studies, different methods and different countries, and they are hard to compare. That is itself telling. The European Parliament study notes that evidence disaggregated beyond gender “remains scarce”, so that “the women most affected are the least visible in the evidence on which policy relies”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-2">9</a></sup> Most research on online abuse measures psychological harm, which is serious and well documented. Economic harm is recorded less often because it is scattered: missed work, the cost of legal and specialist support, counselling, and in some cases the loss of a home.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-3">9</a></sup>

The systems involved also see only fragments. A platform records content decisions and reports. A bank records transactions. Neither is designed to record the pattern of abuse that links them, or the income that a woman loses along the way. This creates a problem of evidence as well as of remedy: the person affected may have to explain one connected experience to several disconnected organisations.

The absence of systematic economic data is itself a governance problem. In earlier work on who bears the cost of unresolved accountability gaps, I show how economic losses can be left unallocated or shifted onto those least able to bear them.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-11" id="fnref-11-1">11</a></sup> The same question arises here: when the financial consequences of digital violence are not systematically recorded, institutions cannot establish who bears the cost, or whether existing arrangements provide meaningful remedy. Without consistent data on lost income, employment, business opportunities and financial independence, policymakers cannot assess whether safeguards and remedies work. Economic harm can stay invisible because no single institution is responsible for measuring its full extent.

The forms of abuse also overlap. Danish research found that 79% of women who had experienced economic abuse by a partner had also experienced technology-facilitated violence.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-4">9</a></sup> Economic abuse can operate through the financial tools themselves. Shared accounts, platform-linked payment features and connected financial services may be used for monitoring, coercion and control, extending technology-facilitated abuse into women’s financial lives. Such abuse can be hard to identify when each institution assesses transactions or account activity on its own.

The stakes are rising in the Global South. Financial services increasingly run through mobile phones, and women in low- and middle-income countries are still 12% less likely than men to use mobile internet; 810 million women do not use it at all.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-12" id="fnref-12-1">12</a></sup> In the Middle East and North Africa, 42% of women own a bank account, against 57% of men. Some programmes already treat the two risks together: in Jordan, UNDP has paired e-wallets and mobile money for women with training on cybersecurity and gender-based violence prevention.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-13" id="fnref-13-1">13</a></sup> For women who are online, the phone is often also the shop, the bank and the marketplace. Abuse that drives them offline can also drive them out of the digital economy.

## Accountability that stops at the platform’s edge

Regulation has begun to recognise these harms. Under the European Union’s Digital Services Act, very large online platforms must assess “any actual or foreseeable negative effects in relation to gender-based violence” and put in place “reasonable, proportionate and effective mitigation measures”. The Act also requires complaint-handling systems and access to out-of-court dispute settlement.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-14" id="fnref-14-1">14</a></sup> Beyond Europe, seven economies introduced cyberharassment laws between October 2023 and October 2025, typically with remedies such as content removal or restrictions on digital contact. Yet even where laws on violence against women exist, expert assessments find their enforcement ineffective in roughly 80% of cases.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-10" id="fnref-10-2">10</a></sup>

These obligations attach to each platform. The payment systems and banks through which abuse can continue fall under separate regimes, with separate rules on evidence, complaints and redress. Research on coordinated “pile-on” harassment describes conduct “whose cumulative effect produces harm that no single actor’s contribution would independently cause”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-5">9</a></sup> When responsibility is organised channel by channel, harm that arises from the combination falls between them, and it can “leave victims without clear avenues for redress”.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-9" id="fnref-9-6">9</a></sup> The incentives can also run the wrong way. A UNESCO-commissioned study of online violence against women journalists found that recommendation algorithms designed to maximise engagement can escalate abuse.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-15" id="fnref-15-1">15</a></sup> Where engagement-driven systems monetise attention, abusive or coordinated content can generate revenue, so one system profits from a harm that another system carries to the woman targeted.

Perpetrators cause the harm. Platforms, payment providers and banks are not automatically responsible for every resulting loss, but they can be required to account for how they assess risks, respond to reports and enable remedy, and the regulators that oversee online safety and financial services each hold part of the picture. In a <a href="/working-papers/#wp-2026-06">recent CTS working paper</a>, I use *interactionality* to describe the condition in which interactions among technological, algorithmic and institutional systems materially shape, amplify, transform or generate outcomes that cannot be adequately understood by examining the participating systems independently.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-16" id="fnref-16-1">16</a></sup>

Interactionality matters because accountability is currently designed one system at a time. A platform can meet its risk-assessment duties, and a bank can process only valid payments, while the abuse continues across both. The same holds in multi-agent AI. My KYS: Know Your Swarm framework addresses accountability for interacting AI agents, and its fairness extension shows that discrimination can emerge from interactions among individually compliant systems that each pass their own fairness checks.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-17" id="fnref-17-1">17</a></sup> The working paper distinguishes two gaps. An Interaction-Level Accountability Gap arises when interactions among systems materially shape or amplify a consequential outcome that accountability focused on each system individually cannot adequately explain or assign. A Cross-System Accountability Gap arises when an outcome crosses a governance boundary but the evidence, authority or access to remedy needed for accountability is not preserved across it. Abuse that moves from social media into payments is an example of the second. Closing these gaps means assessing risk, sharing evidence and designing remedy at the level where systems interact, alongside the level of each system.

<aside class="evalbox" aria-label="Three questions for accountability beyond the platform">
<p class="evalbox__title">Interactionality in practice: three questions for accountability beyond the platform</p>
<ol>
<li><p class="evalbox__q">Traceability: what happened?</p><p>Can the institutions involved establish how the abuse spread and persisted across channels (cross-system interaction and outcome traceability), without requiring the person affected to reconstruct every incident alone?</p></li>
<li><p class="evalbox__q">Accountability: who is responsible when safeguards fail?</p><p>What authority did each institution have to act at each stage, could it intervene meaningfully to interrupt the harm (delegated authority and meaningful intervention), and can each show that it assessed foreseeable risks and acted on repeated reports?</p></li>
<li><p class="evalbox__q">Effective remedy: what happens after the harm?</p><p>Is remedy reachable across systems, so that the person harmed obtains a response that recognises lost income, employment or opportunities, rather than being sent from one complaints process to another?</p></li>
</ol>
</aside>

These questions leave liability to the applicable law. Their purpose is to test whether the arrangements for identifying, stopping and remedying a connected harm are themselves connected.

Accountability must therefore go beyond content removal. Platforms should be able to demonstrate how they identify risks, respond to abuse and enable effective remedies, recognising that economic harm may persist after the content is gone. Because the harm travels, the same must be asked of the banks, payment providers and regulators it passes through.

## Extending accountability beyond the platform

Four steps would help close the gap.

1. **Recognise economic harm in risk assessments:** Platform risk assessments could consider how abuse migrates into financial channels and what economic harm follows, and financial institutions could treat abusive payments as a recognised risk. The Australian banks show that financial institutions can act when they look for the pattern. Economic and financial consequences should run through risk assessments, reporting and remedy mechanisms as a harm in their own right. That includes assessing whether monetisation and engagement systems reward abusive or coordinated content, and whether financial features and account restrictions can contribute to lost income, financial control or exclusion from economic participation.

2. **Connect reports across channels:** A woman who reports abuse to a platform should not have to rebuild her case from scratch with her bank, her phone provider and the police. Regulators responsible for online safety and for financial services can develop carefully bounded pathways for referrals and evidence preservation, with survivor-centred safeguards: consent where appropriate, data minimisation and protection against further disclosure or retaliation. Australia shows what this can achieve. Since 2020, the Commonwealth Bank has blocked more than one million abusive payment messages.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-18" id="fnref-18-1">18</a></sup> In 2021, the national financial intelligence agency, AUSTRAC, issued guidance to help banks identify and report abuse through payment text fields, which led to more suspicious matter reports and referrals to police.<sup class="fn"><a href="#fn-19" id="fnref-19-1">19</a></sup>

3. **Make remedy reach economic loss:** Removing content does not return lost income or a lost job. Some losses are lasting: an interrupted career may be hard to restart, and a public role abandoned under sustained attack may never be regained. Where an account or its monetisation was restricted wrongfully or as a result of abuse, remedy should include restoring them promptly. Redress mechanisms should be able to address economic consequences, and the burden of identifying which system failed should not fall on the person harmed.

4. **Measure what is lost:** Governments, regulators, platforms, financial institutions and researchers should systematically collect and report evidence of the economic and financial consequences of technology-facilitated gender-based violence: lost earnings and employment, business disruption, direct costs such as legal assistance and counselling, financial coercion and control, economic losses linked to AI-generated image-based abuse, and longer-term effects on women’s economic participation. Surveys, complaint mechanisms and incident reporting should capture these consequences alongside the abuse itself, disaggregated by gender, income and region where safe and feasible. Collection should be proportionate and privacy-protecting, with consent where appropriate and aggregated reporting wherever possible, so that better evidence never means sharing survivors’ financial records more widely. With it, policymakers can estimate the economic burden, see where protections fail and who bears the cost, and test whether remedies address the losses women actually experience.

<figure class="insight-quote"><blockquote><p>Removing content does not return lost income or a lost job.</p></blockquote></figure>

Digital platforms and digital finance can widen women’s economic participation, from mobile payments to online businesses and public careers. As finance shifts from digital to autonomous, with AI agents increasingly initiating payments and making decisions, the number of interacting systems that harm can travel through is likely to grow. Keeping the promise of digital finance depends on what interactionality calls for: accountability that extends across interacting systems and institutional boundaries, following harm wherever it travels. Without it, the financial and economic consequences of digital violence risk remaining fragmented, undercounted and borne by the women affected.
